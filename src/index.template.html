How the sound is made

Strings. Each of the six strings is a digital waveguide: a delay line one period long that a wave runs around, losing a little energy and treble on every trip. Two waveguides per string model the two directions a string can swing. The one pushing into the soundboard drains fast and makes the bright attack; the one parallel to the top rings on. Their slight tuning difference gives real guitars their slow beating.

Pick. The excitation is a force pulse whose length depends on where you pick along the string, which cuts out the harmonics that have a node there. Harder strokes give a sharper pulse and a brighter attack, and a hard pluck briefly raises string tension, so the note starts a few cents sharp and settles.

Body. The strings drive a bank of resonant modes: the air resonance near 100 Hz, the top-plate pair near 190 and 205 Hz, then dozens of denser, weaker modes up to 7 kHz, heard from two mic positions. Below the air resonance the top and the soundhole cancel, so the low E fundamental is quieter than its overtones, as on a real guitar. Undamped strings pick up energy through the bridge and ring sympathetically.

Calibration. Decay times per harmonic, inharmonicity and the brightness curve over time were fitted against recordings of real steel-string guitar notes.

Hand. Notes that arrive together become a chord, placed on the neck with a fingering search (span, barres, open strings, voice leading). A pick stroke reaches the strings in order with timing set by velocity, string gauge and a little human scatter. Strum direction follows a pendulum: the hand swings every subdivision, so the gap since your last chord decides whether this one is a down or an upstroke.
