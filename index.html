How the sound is made

Strings. Each string is the sum of its own vibration modes, two per harmonic, one for each direction the string can swing. A mode's pitch includes the stretch from the string's bending stiffness; its decay comes from measured string losses (Woodhouse's model for bronze and nylon strings, with older strings losing more treble).

Bridge. The string's energy leaks into the guitar through the bridge, and how fast depends on how easily the bridge moves at that exact frequency. A harmonic that lands on a body resonance drains in a fraction of a second; one that lands between resonances rings for many seconds. The bridge also pulls each harmonic's pitch slightly and moves differently along and across the top, so the two swing directions split by a fraction of a hertz to a few hertz and beat. That is why every harmonic decays at its own rate, fades, comes back, and crosses its neighbors, which is most of what makes a real note sound alive.

Body. The same body drives the sound: an air resonance near 100 Hz, the top plate pair near 195 and 205 Hz, dipole modes that radiate less, then hundreds of denser modes spaced about 38 Hz apart up to 9 kHz, each moving the bridge at its own angle (Woodhouse's statistical model, scaled to measured guitar admittance, about −45 dB re 1 m/Ns). Two mic positions hear the modes with different weights.

Pick. The pick pushes the string sideways, scrapes along it for a few milliseconds, then slips off. The pluck point carves notches into the harmonic series, the pick width and slip time set the brightness, and the push direction sets the balance of the two swing directions. While loaded, the string presses the bridge sideways; that force vanishes at release, kicking the body's air and top modes — the soft thump under every note. Hard plucks briefly raise string tension, so notes start a few cents sharp, and they excite the string's lengthwise resonances (around 1.4 kHz on the low E), part of a steel string's metallic edge.

Wood. Each species enters as measured density, stiffness along and across the grain, and internal damping. What that does to the sound depends on how the guitar is built: a luthier thins each plate to the same stiffness, so a lighter wood mostly makes the top lighter; a factory keeps thickness fixed, so stiffness and mass both change. Every body mode is shifted by its share of top and back motion. The effects are real but small, as blind tests of tonewoods have found: a few percent in mode frequency, one to three decibels in level.

The player. Held against the torso, the back can't ring freely: back-plate modes lose about half their Q, the air and top resonances about a quarter, and the player's body absorbs what the back radiates. On a stand, the back sings.

Microphones. The guitar radiates from several places at once: the soundhole, the bridge, the two halves of the lower bout, below the bridge, the upper bout and the back. Each body mode drives them in its own pattern (the air mode through the soundhole, dipole modes with halves in opposite phase). Each mic hears every region at its true distance and angle, through its polar pattern, with the bass boost directional mics get up close, and with regions on the far side of the body heard only by diffraction.

The room. Reflections are traced by the image-source method: each wall bounce is attenuated per octave by its material, per ray by the direction it left the guitar (the top radiates forward, the back backward, the player absorbs) and by the direction it reaches the mic. A late tail follows, decaying per octave at the Eyring rate for the room's volume and surfaces, at the energy level room acoustics predicts, so moving the mics away naturally trades direct sound for room sound.

Calibration. Decay per harmonic, inharmonicity, brightness over time, beating depth, and strum timing and brightness were measured from recordings of real steel-string guitars, single notes and labeled down and up strums, and the model was checked against them.

Hand. Notes that arrive together become a chord, placed on the neck with a fingering search (span, barres, open strings, voice leading). A downstroke crosses the strings in about 35–55 ms, as measured strums do; upstrokes are quicker, catch mostly the treble strings, and come out slightly brighter. Direction follows a pendulum: the hand swings every subdivision, so the gap since your last chord decides down or up.
